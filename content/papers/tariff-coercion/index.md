##### Abstract


The success of an economic sanction is usually measured by whether the sender's objectives were attained, although an objective can be attained without any concession by the sanctioned government. I code the observable concessions of target governments in 193 trade sanctions imposed by single states between 1949 and 2019 and compare them with the graded attainment that the Global Sanctions Data Base records for the same episodes. I find that targets that depend more on the sender, relative to the sender's dependence on them, are less likely to concede at all, while the attainment of objectives shows no such association, and on that margin the difference between the two measures is statistically clear. In the model I develop, concessions are graded under an imposed sanction and all or nothing at the threat stage, and in the equilibria in which one type of sender is indifferent over imposition, that indifference fixes what an imposed sanction is expected to extract. In those equilibria the damage to the target changes how often sanctions are imposed and how concessions divide between partial and full, while the sender's cost determines their expected size. However, threat-stage data do not show the selection through which leverage would lower concessions among imposed sanctions, and the sanction instruments restrict only a small share of bilateral trade.
