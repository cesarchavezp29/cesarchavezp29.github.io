##### Abstract

I study how reported intimate partner violence changes when formal mines in Peru begin to produce. Combining nineteen years of Demographic and Family Health Survey data with monthly production records and operating histories verified mine by mine, I estimate cohort-specific difference-in-differences effects around nine openings within twenty kilometres of district centres. Inference relies on spatial placebo reassignments of the openings over an assignment space that includes the observed mine locations. Over up to six fully post-onset survey years, reported past-year physical or sexual violence changes by −0.1 percentage points on a baseline of 15.2 percent, with a placebo reference interval from −4.7 to +4.4 points. The interval is inconsistent with large constant effects but remains compatible with economically meaningful changes in either direction. Night lights rise within thirty kilometres of the openings, while women's employment and household incomes are imprecisely estimated. For the projects with a documented construction date, construction began one to three years before production and night lights began to rise with it, so the estimates measure the change at the start of production and not the full effect of a mine.
